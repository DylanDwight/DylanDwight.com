Overview

Shoreline Electric is a licensed & insured electrician on the Oregon Coast, in its first year of business. It does residential, commercial, and remodel work from Cannon Beach to Pacific City.

It’s also my family’s business. My dad owns it. That made this a real paid job with a real client, but one I had unusual access to, so I’ve been careful to report only what I measured.

The old site was a Canva template. It looked good on a big screen. On a phone it took 15.5 seconds to show its main content, the page weighed 13.3 MB, the phone number wasn’t tappable, and the icon links had no labels for screen readers. The business didn’t own the code, and the site lived on a Canva subscription.

I redesigned the site around one job, getting customers to reach the business, then built it from scratch in code & moved the domain off Canva without touching the company email.

UX Research & Discovery

Someone with a dead outlet or a tripped panel wants three answers, fast: Do you do this kind of work? Do you come to my town? How do I reach you?

I researched other electricians’ websites to see how trades businesses present their services, service areas, and contact options. Then I went through the Canva site the way a customer would, on a phone, and ran baseline tests before I changed anything.

What the Old Site Showed

  • The navigation was two underlined text links, Services & Contact, floating over the video.
  • The phone number was plain text, written “(971)456-1800.” On a phone you couldn’t tap it to call.
  • White text sat right on a bright beach video with nothing behind it.
  • Six images had no alt text, and four icon links had no name for screen readers.
  • A leftover Canva placeholder, “Your paragraph text,” was live on the page.
  • No structured data, so Google had to guess the business details.

Behind the Scenes

The baseline report showed where the 13.3 MB went: two background videos (12.5 MB & 7.3 MB files), a 2 MB Canva script, and six or more font files. Most of the business’s work comes in by phone, and customers text, call, or email. So every decision got judged on one question: does this make reaching the business easier?

The Customer Path I Designed Around

  1. 1FindSearch for an electrician on Google or Maps
  2. 2CheckDo they do this work? Are they licensed?
  3. 3ConfirmDo they come to my town?
  4. 4Reach outCall, text, or email in one tap

Design & Development Approach

Iteration 1: A Faithful Rebuild

The first brief was a rebuild, not a redesign: same layout, copy, photos, and video, just fast, accessible, and owned by the business. I built that version first.

Seeing it next to the original made the real problem clear. Fixing what visitors can’t see wasn’t enough. The layout itself still had no clear place to call, and it still read like a template. So I changed direction to a custom redesign, and I kept the rebuild on its own branch as a fallback.

Iterating on the Redesign

  • An illustrated coast scene replaced the video in the hero. It didn’t feel like the business, so the original drone video came back, darker & compressed.
  • The first heading font was too wide. Barlow Condensed matched the lettering on the real van much better.
  • The service area went from a paragraph of towns to a Highway 101 road sign.
  • A road strip with the work van went along the bottom of the screen & got simpler with each pass: a town sign, tick marks, then just the road, a guardrail, and the coast’s landmark rocks.

Visual Direction: Drawn From the Van

The business’s strongest brand piece isn’t its website. It’s the white work van with the orange script lettering that drives around the coast every day. I drew the van from a photo, panel by panel, and pulled the site’s look from it: white & dark navy panels, the orange script, bold condensed caps, and amber for every action.

On the live site, the van drives along a strip of coast road at the bottom of the screen as you scroll. It passes Haystack Rock, Twin Rocks, and Three Arch Rocks near their towns, and reaches the end of the road at the contact section. Scroll back up and it turns around to show its driver side. It’s decorative, so screen readers skip it, and the turnaround animation is switched off for anyone who has reduced motion turned on.

The Build

Why Astro

Astro lets me write plain HTML & CSS in small components, then builds them into ordinary static pages. It ships no JavaScript unless I ask for it, so the page loads fast by default. All the business facts (phone, email, towns, services) live in one data file, so they’re the same everywhere they appear.

From the first build on, every round of changes went in as a pull request with its own private preview link: 15 in all between October 25 & 28, each one checked on a preview before it went live.

Accessibility: WCAG 2.2 AA

  • Contrast over the drone video, measured from screenshots taken every 3 seconds of the clip: white text at 9:1 or better, the amber word at 4.6:1 or better.
  • A skip link, a visible focus style on every link & button, and nothing hidden under the sticky header.
  • A pause button on the video, which never autoplays with reduced motion turned on.
  • Tap targets at least 24 px, and no sideways scrolling from 360 px phones to 1920 px desktops.
  • No layout shift as the fonts load (CLS 0).

What WAVE Taught Me

My first WAVE run on the new site found 4 errors & 11 contrast errors. The 4 errors were decorative rock drawings missing an “empty alt” marker. The 11 contrast errors were the 11 town names on the highway sign.

The towns flip onto the sign when it scrolls into view. Before that they waited at 0 opacity, and WAVE checks the page frozen at load, so it saw invisible text. I changed the starting state so the towns wait turned edge-on at full color instead. They still look the same when they flip in, and they’re readable to every tool & screen reader. The final run: 0 errors, 0 contrast errors.

Lesson: automated checkers test the page frozen at load, so an animation’s hidden starting state counts as real content.

Moving Off Canva Without Breaking Email

The riskiest part of the project wasn’t the design. It was the domain. The company email runs on the same domain as the website, and one wrong DNS change would stop it.

Before touching anything, I looked up every DNS record & saved a copy of the email ones. At launch, only the records that pointed at Canva changed.

RecordWhat it doesAt launch
A record (main domain)Points the domain at the websiteChanged from Canva to Vercel
wwwHandles “www.” in front of the domainPointed at Vercel, which redirects it to the main domain
Canva verificationProved the domain to CanvaRemoved
MX, SPF, DKIM, DMARCDeliver company email & keep it out of spamNot touched

Vercel issued the HTTPS certificate, and the company inbox sent & received email after the switch. The business now owns its code, and nothing depends on a Canva account.

Local SEO: Getting Found

A local electrician gets found by town. The site needed to tell Google, in a format it can read, who the business is & where it works.

Search

  • A page title & description written around the service & the coast.
  • Electrician schema (structured data) with the name, phone, email, logo, and all 11 towns.
  • It’s a service-area business, so the schema lists towns, not a street address.
  • A sitemap, robots.txt, and a 404 page Google won’t index.

Sharing

  • A designed preview image for when the link is texted or posted on Facebook.
  • A shorter share title, because text message previews cut off the full one.
  • Browser & phone home-screen icons made from the logo.
  • The same name & phone number everywhere on the page.

Why the Old Site’s Perfect WAVE Score Didn’t Count

WAVE gave the Canva site 0 errors & an AIM score of 10 out of 10. But its own alerts said “No heading structure” & “No page regions,” and the page showed up blank inside WAVE. It was scoring an empty page, not the real content. That’s why I lean on PageSpeed’s accessibility audit for the before numbers.

What I’m Not Claiming

The site went live at the end of October 2026, so it’s too early to say whether it brings in more calls. I haven’t measured calls, jobs, or revenue, and I’m not claiming any change in them.

Speed & accessibility are what this project changed, and they’re what I measured.

Key Takeaway

A trades website has one job: help someone reach the business, fast, on a phone. The old site looked fine & did that job badly. Making it 9× faster to show its main content & putting call, text, and email on the first screen did more for that job than any visual flourish. The van is the fun part, but the Call button is the point.

Reflection & Next Steps

What I Learned

Building the faithful rebuild first was worth it, even though we moved past it. It proved the speed & accessibility fixes on their own, and putting it next to the original is what showed the layout needed to change too.

This was my first domain & DNS migration. Writing down every email record before changing anything turned the scariest step into a short checklist. It was also my first Astro project. Keeping every business fact in one data file is a habit I’ll bring to every small business site.

Working for family made it easy to skip the paperwork. I didn’t send a written estimate before starting, and I should have. The next client gets a scope & estimate before any work begins.
